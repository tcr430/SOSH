# The feature I’d bet the company on

If I had to choose one truly differentiating capability, it would be:

An Opportunity Engine rather than a Publishing Engine.

Every morning, the user opens the app and sees something like:

- “Three conversations in your niche are taking off.”
- “A competitor’s launch is getting traction—here’s the angle they missed.”
- “Twenty customers have complained about the same issue this week. Turn it into a post.”
- “This tweet from an influencer is likely to go viral. Reply with this perspective in the next 15 minutes.”
- “Your best-performing topic from last year is trending again. Here’s an updated version.”

Current tools ask, “What do you want to publish?”

A market-changing tool asks, “Here are the highest-leverage actions you can take right now to grow.”

That shifts the product from being a scheduler into an always-on growth advisor. Combined with autonomous execution (drafting, adapting for each platform, scheduling, and engagement), it becomes much harder for competitors to replicate because its value compounds with the user’s history, brand knowledge, and network effects rather than just offering another content calendar.

# One man startup

Ironically, I think a one-person startup has a better chance than an established company.

Hootsuite, Buffer, Sprout Social, etc. have spent years optimizing around publishing workflows. Their customers expect calendars, approvals, permissions, reports, and enterprise features. They can’t easily pivot to “AI growth agent” without disrupting their core business.

As a solo founder, your advantage is that you don’t have to build a platform. You only need to build one thing that users open every day.

Here’s how I’d approach it.

## Phase 1: Forget publishing

Don’t build:

- Scheduler
- Calendar
- Analytics
- Media library
- Teams
- Permissions
- Approval flows

Those are expensive to build and don’t differentiate you.

Instead, build a browser-based dashboard that asks:

> “What should I do today?”

The first version doesn’t even need to publish posts.

# If I were starting this tomorrow

I would resist the temptation to build a “better Hootsuite.” Instead, I’d build this sequence:

## 1. Opportunity Feed – “Here’s what you should talk about today.”
## 2. One-click content generation from those opportunities.
## 3. Learning loop that explains why posts succeed or fail.
## 4. Brand memory that accumulates knowledge from your connected tools.
## 5. Autonomous execution (drafts, scheduling, engagement suggestions, eventually publishing).

The key insight is that you don’t have to replace Hootsuite on day one. You only need to become the app people open before they open Hootsuite. Once users rely on you to decide what to post, adding scheduling later is straightforward. Going in the opposite direction—from a scheduler to an intelligent growth assistant—is much harder.

# Where I think the real opportunity is

I actually wouldn’t compete with Hootsuite directly.

I’d compete with the blank page.

Every creator, founder, and marketer has the same daily problem:

“I know I should post something… but what?”

If your product reliably answers that question in under a minute, you’ve solved the highest-friction part of the workflow. Publishing can always come later, or users can continue using Buffer, Hootsuite, or native platform schedulers.

That lets you stay small while building something users return to every day. Once that habit exists, you can gradually expand into publishing, analytics, engagement, and other capabilities. The key is earning the daily habit first, rather than trying to replace an entire social media management suite from day one.

# Calendars and publishing

I actually agree. I think there’s a trap in startup advice where people say, “Don’t build features that already exist.” That’s only half true.

Calendars, editors, and publishers absolutely add value. The question is whether they’re your differentiator.

Think about it this way:

- Canva didn’t win because it invented image editing.
- Notion didn’t win because it invented documents.
- Linear didn’t win because it invented issue tracking.

They won because they made the core workflow dramatically better while still including the expected features.

The same can apply here.

I’d build the product around three pillars

## 1. Create (Editor)

A genuinely AI-native editor.

Not “click AI to generate.”

More like:

- Rewrite this paragraph for founders.
- Turn this into a carousel.
- Show me three stronger hooks.
- Find claims that need sources.
- Suggest screenshots to include.
- Detect repetitive posts from my history.
- Tell me why this won’t perform.

Think of it as Cursor for social content.

---

## 2. Plan (Calendar)

Calendars haven’t evolved much.

Imagine if the calendar answered questions instead of just storing posts.

For example:

You’re posting product updates three days in a row.

You haven’t posted educational content in 10 days.

Thursday has historically been your best day for this audience.

This launch conflicts with WWDC.

This topic is becoming stale.

The calendar becomes a strategic planning tool rather than a grid of scheduled posts.

---

## 3. Execute (Publisher)

Publishing still matters.

Especially if you support:

- platform-native previews
- first comment scheduling
- threaded posts
- approval workflows (later)
- retry logic
- queue management
- bulk publishing
- media optimization
- timezone intelligence

People expect this.

If you don’t have it eventually, many won’t switch.
